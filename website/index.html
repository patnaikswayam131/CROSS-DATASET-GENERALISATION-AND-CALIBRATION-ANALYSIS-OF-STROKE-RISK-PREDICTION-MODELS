Problem Statement

The Benchmark Illusion

Machine learning models for stroke prediction are typically evaluated on held-out splits of the same dataset they were trained on. This creates an illusion of performance: the model never encounters a distribution it has not already learned. In clinical deployment, however, the target population is almost never the same as the training population. Age distributions differ, measurement protocols vary, and disease definitions change between institutions.

Dataset shift occurs when the joint distribution P(X,Y) differs between training and deployment. This encompasses covariate shift (different feature distributions), prior probability shift (different class prevalence), and concept shift (different relationships between features and outcomes). Standard held-out evaluation, no matter how rigorous the train-test split, cannot detect any of these failure modes because both sets are drawn from the same source.

For rare events like stroke (4-5% prevalence), ROC-AUC is misleadingly optimistic. It credits the model for correctly identifying the overwhelming majority of non-stroke cases, which any trivial classifier can achieve. PR-AUC, by contrast, evaluates only the precision-recall trade-off for the minority class. When a model achieves PR-AUC of 0.077 on the target population, it offers only 1.9x the performance of a random classifier -- a finding that ROC-AUC of 0.709 completely conceals.

Prior Studies

Literature Review

The existing work on machine learning for stroke prediction spans three thematic areas: benchmark model development, class imbalance management, and cross-dataset generalisation.

2.1 Stroke Prediction and In-Domain Evaluation

Multiple studies achieve high in-domain ROC-AUC (0.80-0.98) on the Kaggle Stroke Prediction Dataset but share a critical limitation: evaluation is restricted to held-out splits of the same source population.

Sutcu et al. (2025)

Multi-method Kaggle analysis; XGBoost is best-performing classifier on in-domain metrics.

Chakraborty et al. (2024)

98.6% accuracy via stacked ensemble + PCA dimensionality reduction.

Hassan et al. (2024)

Identifies age, glucose, and cardiovascular history as dominant predictors.

Chun et al. (2021)

Stroke risk models from 0.5 million Chinese adults; evaluation restricted to development population.

Huang and Liu (2025)

NHANES-based workflow -- trains and validates within the same survey cycle without external testing.

Saleem et al. (2025)

CBDA-ResNet50 with augmentation improves minority-class recall, same in-domain evaluation constraint.

2.2 Class Imbalance in Clinical Prediction

Stroke datasets present fewer than 5% positive cases. Balancing methods improve recall but can distort calibration -- a confound present before any domain shift occurs.

Melnykova et al. (2025)

Standard classifiers fail stroke-case detection under imbalance; recommends cost-sensitive alternatives.

Araf et al. (2024)

Cost-sensitive learning improves recall but may compromise calibration quality.

Salmi et al. (2024)

10-year systematic review: ROC-AUC routinely masks minority-class detection failure.

Carriero et al. (2024)

Simulation shows imbalance corrections can harm performance -- especially distorting predicted probabilities.

Sheetal et al. (2025)

Cardiovascular prediction: high accuracy co-exists with clinically significant failure on positive cases.

2.3 Cross-Dataset Generalisation and Domain Adaptation

Domain adaptation methods achieve modest AUC recovery under moderate covariate shift, but assume covariate shift as the primary mechanism -- a premise this study directly challenges through concept shift analysis.

Silva et al. (2025)

Systematic review of dataset shift detection; identifies PSI and KS tests as operationally useful diagnostics.

Lee et al. (2023)

Standard ERM produces fragile EHR models; proposes stable prediction under distributional change.

Ryu et al. (2022)

Same model across emergency departments shows site-to-site performance variation despite identical weights.

Guo et al. (2021)

Temporal shift within a single institute degrades output beyond what label shift alone explains.

Lu et al. (2025)

PSI values may be dominated by measurement protocol variation rather than demographic change.

Fan et al. (2025)

Stroke survival models show substantial degradation outside the development site.

Akinwumi et al. (2025)

Metric selection substantially changes conclusions about relative model utility.

Motivation and Research Gaps

What Remains Unaddressed

[i] Motivation: Models trained on the Kaggle Stroke Prediction Dataset have shown ROC-AUC scores up to 0.98. These figures are cited as supporting evidence for clinical utility -- but such metrics cannot determine if learned decision boundaries are valid beyond the training distribution. Clinical decision support systems encounter patients with different demographic compositions, measurement instruments, and institutional practices. This gap between controlled benchmark evaluation and real-world deployment motivates a direct empirical test of generalisation on an independent, nationally representative cohort.
Gap 1

No Cross-Population External Validation

No prior study has evaluated Kaggle-trained stroke models against a nationally representative independent population survey. Even large-scale cohort studies evaluate only within the development population. Held-out in-distribution evaluation cannot determine whether learned patterns transfer to populations with different demographics, sampling methods, or clinical measurement protocols.

Held-out accuracy is not external validity
Gap 2

ROC-AUC Dominates Despite Known Limitations

ROC-AUC dominates evaluation in this field, yet under severe class imbalance it is well established that ROC-AUC masks substantial minority-class detection failure. For classifiers operating at 4-5% stroke prevalence, PR-AUC offers a more clinically meaningful criterion -- and studies reporting both metrics find that rankings and conclusions can differ markedly.

PR-AUC is the appropriate metric for rare-event tasks
Gap 3

Measurement Protocol Mismatches Treated as Details

Measurement protocol differences between datasets are routinely treated as administrative details rather than scientific confounds. The glucose variable demonstrates this risk: NHANES records fasting plasma glucose under standardised conditions, while Kaggle's average glucose level is of unspecified collection type. The resulting PSI of 1.495 cannot be remediated by any adaptation technique.

PSI = 1.495 for glucose -- not correctable, must be documented

Methodology

Experimental Protocol

This study follows a source-only training protocol: all three classifiers were trained exclusively on the Kaggle Stroke Prediction Dataset and evaluated on NHANES 2021-2023 without any retraining, fine-tuning, or domain adaptation. This simulates the real-world scenario where a model developed on one dataset is deployed directly to a new clinical population.
The Kaggle Stroke Prediction Dataset (N=5,109, stroke prevalence 4.87%) served as the source domain. NHANES 2021-2023 (N=3,167, stroke prevalence 4.10%) served as the target domain. NHANES is a nationally representative health survey conducted by the CDC using stratified, multi-stage probability sampling.
Four features were harmonised across datasets: age, gender, BMI, and average glucose level. The primary confound is glucose measurement -- Kaggle contains unspecified glucose readings while NHANES reports fasting plasma glucose (LBXGLU). This definitional mismatch is the primary source of the 1.495 PSI observed for glucose.
Three classifiers were evaluated: Logistic Regression (C=1.0, max_iter=1000), Random Forest (n_estimators=200, max_depth=10, min_samples_leaf=5), and XGBoost (n_estimators=200, max_depth=4, learning_rate=0.1, scale_pos_weight=19.5). SMOTE oversampling was applied to the training fold only.
Models were evaluated using ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, Brier score, and Expected Calibration Error (ECE, 10 bins). 95% confidence intervals were computed via 1,000-iteration stratified bootstrap resampling. Non-overlapping CIs between Kaggle and NHANES indicate statistically significant degradation.
Distribution shift was quantified using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test for statistical significance and Population Stability Index (PSI) for magnitude. PSI thresholds: <0.10 stable, 0.10-0.25 moderate, >0.25 severe. A logistic regression domain classifier achieved 82.1% accuracy (random baseline: 50%), confirming the datasets are distinguishable.
Feature patching replaces one feature at a time in the NHANES test set with values from the Kaggle distribution, then re-evaluates model performance. If performance improves (positive delta), the shift in that feature was the cause of degradation. All-negative deltas indicate concurrent concept shift: the learned feature-outcome relationships have changed across populations.
Platt scaling (logistic recalibration) and isotonic regression were applied as post-hoc calibration methods. Platt scaling produced no change for any model. Isotonic regression was neutral for Logistic Regression, caused a -0.037 drop for Random Forest, and a catastrophic -0.130 drop for XGBoost (0.709 to 0.579), demonstrating that miscalibration under domain shift is not recoverable by post-hoc methods.

Mathematical Formulation

Formal Definitions

Mathematical definitions of all key quantities used in this study. All metrics include 95% bootstrap confidence intervals computed over 1,000 stratified resamples.

Notation Reference (Table 2)
SSource domain (Kaggle Stroke Prediction Dataset)
TTarget domain (NHANES 2021-2023)
f_SClassifier trained on source domain S only
P(X)Marginal feature distribution
P(Y|X)Conditional label distribution (stroke given features)
M(f,D)Performance metric of model f on dataset D
Ei, AiExpected (Kaggle) and Actual (NHANES) proportion in PSI bin i
Pn, RnPrecision and recall at threshold n
SkSet of predictions falling in calibration bin k
BNumber of bootstrap resamples (B = 1,000)
theta_bMetric value on b-th bootstrap resample
Eq. 1
Covariate Shift

Covariate shift occurs when the marginal feature distribution differs between source and target domains while the conditional label distribution remains stable.

$$P_S(X) \neq P_T(X) \quad \text{but} \quad P_S(Y|X) = P_T(Y|X)$$

S = source domain (Kaggle), T = target domain (NHANES)

Eq. 2
Concept Shift

Concept shift occurs when the relationship between features and labels differs across domains. The decision boundary itself has changed.

$$P_S(Y|X) \neq P_T(Y|X)$$

All-negative feature-patching deltas confirm concurrent concept shift in this study

Eq. 3
Population Stability Index (PSI)

Quantifies distributional shift magnitude using binned proportions. Thresholds: <0.10 stable, 0.10-0.20 moderate, >0.20 severe.

$$\text{PSI} = \sum_{i=1}^{M}(E_i - A_i)\ln\!\left(\frac{E_i}{A_i}\right)$$

Ei = Kaggle bin proportion, Ai = NHANES bin proportion, M = 10 bins

Eq. 4
Generalisation Gap

Measures performance loss under domain shift, where the model is trained on source domain only.

$$\Delta = \mathcal{M}(f_{\mathcal{S}}, \mathcal{S}_{\text{test}}) - \mathcal{M}(f_{\mathcal{S}}, \mathcal{T})$$

f_S = model trained on S only, M = performance metric, T = target NHANES

Eq. 5
Average Precision (PR-AUC)

Preferred over ROC-AUC as it is sensitive to minority-class detection failure under class imbalance. The random baseline equals the class prevalence (~0.041 for NHANES).

$$\text{AP} = \sum_{n}(R_n - R_{n-1})P_n$$

Pn = precision at threshold n, Rn = recall at threshold n

Eq. 6
Expected Calibration Error (ECE)

Uses 10 uniform bins. ECE >0.40 means predicted probabilities deviate from true frequencies by over 40 percentage points on average.

$$\text{ECE} = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|S_k|}{n}\left|\text{acc}(S_k)-\text{conf}(S_k)\right|$$

acc(Sk) = fraction of positives in bin, conf(Sk) = mean predicted probability, K = 10

Eq. 7
Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

B = 1,000 stratified resamples (seed = 42). Non-overlapping CIs between Kaggle and NHANES confirm statistically significant degradation.

$$\text{CI}_{95\%} = [\hat{\theta}_{0.025}, \hat{\theta}_{0.975}]$$

theta_b = metric on b-th bootstrap resample, B = 1,000 resamples

Algorithms

Experimental Pipeline Pseudocode

Two algorithms govern the experimental pipeline. Algorithm 1 covers the complete evaluation workflow. Algorithm 2 isolates each feature's contribution to the generalisation gap and provides empirical evidence of concept shift.

Algorithm 1 -- Cross-Domain Generalisation Evaluation 
Input:
  Kaggle dataset, NHANES dataset
  features = {age, gender, bmi, avg_glucose_level}
  classifiers = {Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost}

Output:
  ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, Brier Score, ECE
  (with 95% confidence intervals for both domains)

---------------------------------------------------------
1.  Map shared features across Kaggle and NHANES

2.  Split Kaggle: 70% training, 30% test
      (stratified, seed = 42)

3.  Fit imputer and scaler on Kaggle training set ONLY

4.  Apply fitted imputer and scaler to:
      - Kaggle test set
      - NHANES cohort  <- no parameters re-estimated

5.  for each classifier:
      Train on Kaggle training set with class balancing

6.  for each classifier:
      Generate predicted probabilities on:
        - Kaggle test set
        - NHANES cohort
      Compute ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, Brier Score, ECE
      Compute 95% confidence intervals
        via 1,000 bootstrap resamples (seed = 42)
      Compute generalisation gap
        = Kaggle AUC - NHANES AUC

7.  for each feature:
      Compute KS statistic and PSI
        between Kaggle train and NHANES
      Train domain classifier
        (Kaggle vs. NHANES samples)

8.  for each classifier:
      Apply Platt scaling on Kaggle training set
        evaluate on NHANES
      Apply isotonic regression on Kaggle training set
        evaluate on NHANES

Return: all metrics, confidence intervals,
        shift statistics, adaptation results
Algorithm 2 -- Shift Contribution Analysis (Feature Patching) 
Input:
  Trained XGBoost model  f_S
  Kaggle training set (scaled)
  NHANES test set (scaled)
  NHANES labels

Output:
  Delta AUC per feature when covariate shift is corrected

---------------------------------------------------------
1.  Compute baseline AUC on NHANES using f_S

2.  for each feature f in {age, gender, bmi, glucose}:
      Copy the NHANES test set -> X_patched
      Replace column f in X_patched with values
        sampled from the Kaggle training distribution
      Compute patched_AUC = AUC(f_S, X_patched)
      Compute delta = patched_AUC - baseline_AUC

3.  if ALL delta values are negative:
      -> Concept shift confirmed
        (correcting covariate shift WORSENS performance,
         meaning P_S(Y|X) != P_T(Y|X))

Return:
  feature_name, patched_AUC, delta  for each feature

Key Findings from Comparison

[!] 63% PR-AUC Collapse Invisible to ROC-AUC. Sutcu et al. report gradient boosting ROC-AUC of 0.834 on in-domain holdout. This study achieves 0.838 in-domain and 0.713 cross-domain. [!] High Accuracy Does Not Imply External Validity. The 98.6% in-domain accuracy reported by Chakraborty et al. (2024) on the same Kaggle data reinforces the core finding: held-out accuracy provides no reliable indication of performance on an independent population.

Conclusion

What This Study Demonstrates

Stroke risk classifiers trained on the Kaggle benchmark exhibit statistically significant and practically consequential performance degradation when applied without retraining to NHANES 2021-2023. Feature-patching confirms concurrent concept shift, and post-hoc calibration provides no recovery under these conditions.

External Validation Is Non-Negotiable

In-domain hold-out evaluation is insufficient to establish clinical applicability. External validation against an independent, population-representative dataset is a necessary condition -- not an optional supplement.

Report PR-AUC as Primary Metric

PR-AUC should be reported as the primary metric for rare-event prediction tasks. It is sensitive to the failure mode that domain shift induces while ROC-AUC is not -- and the 51-63% PR-AUC collapse is entirely invisible to ROC-AUC analysis alone.

Document Measurement Protocols Rigorously

Feature-level measurement documentation -- specifying the exact measurement protocol for every variable -- is a methodological prerequisite for valid cross-dataset comparisons. The glucose PSI of 1.495 reflects a definitional mismatch that no adaptation technique can remediate.

Open Research Directions

These findings collectively point toward a standard where clinical prediction models are evaluated and deployed with the rigour applied to pharmaceutical trials -- where independent external validation is a prerequisite, not an afterthought.

[+]

Broader Cohort Coverage

Extend the NHANES cohort to include non-fasting participants and alternative surveys, exposing cross-domain failure patterns across more diverse demographics and measurement contexts.

[+]

Feature Provenance Protocol

Develop a structured harmonisation protocol formally mapping clinical measurement definitions across widely used stroke and cardiovascular benchmarks -- enabling reproducible, scientifically defensible cross-dataset comparisons.

[+]

Representation-Level Adaptation

Post-hoc calibration proves ineffective under concept shift. Density ratio estimation and importance reweighting offer potential cross-domain performance recovery without requiring labelled target data.

[+]

Causal Stability Analysis

Causal stability analysis (Subbaswamy and Saria, 2020) provides a principled path toward identifying features whose relationship with stroke outcomes remains invariant across populations -- enabling models that generalise by construction rather than coincidence.
